No lockup, no greeting. A brand plate over a modal that is about to destroy something reads as a marketing moment. The title carries the room.
Two gates, two steps, never one box. Step 1 proves intent (consequences, typed name); step 2 proves identity (password or code). Stacking both in one dialog makes people type their name and their password to dismiss a box they opened by accident.
The commit button names the action — “Delete account”, not “Confirm”. It is the only filled button, in #C4213C when the action destroys and plum when it does not. The Constitution’s #fa4666 is an error ink: it passes as text or border on light paper but only reaches 3.4:1 as a fill under a white label, so destructive fills use the darkened step.
Cancel is a real button, not a link, sits left, is focused on open, and is worded as the safe outcome (“Keep my account”) on the step that has one.
Forgot-password never navigates. It sends the reset link in place and leaves the dialog and the pending action alive. Routing to /login here loses the action and drops the user on a sign-in card they are already signed in to.
Method, and when to skip it
Password accounts: password. Social-only accounts: an emailed code, not a second trip through the provider — an already-signed-in Google session returns instantly and silently, so the click proves possession of a browser tab, nothing more. If you must use the provider, it has to force a fresh challenge, and that is a redirect out of the flow.
Freshness window. Skip the guard entirely if the session authenticated in the last five minutes; a user who just signed in to delete their account should not be asked twice.
Same shell, different weight. Change email, change password, disconnect a provider, export data: identical dialog, plum button, no consequence panel. Reserve the panel and the red for irreversible loss.
Wrong password is an inline field error, not a toast and not a dismissal. The typed name and the pending action survive the mistake.
Deletion itself is a grace period, not an execution. Signing out with a 30-day window and one “undo” email is a better safety net than any amount of dialog friction — and it lets step 1 stay honest about being reversible until then.

14c What changed, and what else belongs on it

The card in the screenshot is a data dump with decoration. Colour means one thing: shared

Every chip is the neutral surface. Plum is spent only on the languages and interests you also have, and those sort to the front. One glance answers the only question a stranger's card raises: is there anything between us.

Names, not codes

“Spanish”, not ES. Fluent languages lose their chips entirely and become a plain sentence — they are background, not achievement. Chips stay for learning, where the CEFR level is real information.

The card ends in an action

Presence. The green dot — active today. Cheap to compute, and it is what decides whether a message is worth sending.

12d Reduce motion, concretely
Stops
Emblem unfold · blade bloom · particle field · the crest deck fan · page and modal slides · anything that moves an object across the screen or loops.
Stays
Colour changes, 120ms opacity fades, focus rings, toggle knobs. Feedback is not decoration — removing it makes the app feel broken, not calm.
const reduce = matchMedia('(prefers-reduced-motion: reduce)').matches;
html[data-motion="reduced"] * { animation: none; transition-property: opacity, color; }

Read the OS setting on load, let the App tab override it in either direction, write the result to one attribute on <html>. CSS handles everything declarative from there. The two things CSS can't stop are the particle field and the unfold if they run on canvas or a JS timeline — those have to check the flag before they start and render their final frame instead. Same flag gates the first-session deck fan in 10a: a hint that moves is exactly what someone turned this off to avoid.

Why it matters more for you than for most apps: three of your best ideas are animations, and vestibular sensitivity is common enough that a reading product will hit it daily. The switch is what lets you keep the unfold at full confidence.

It was an over-correction: I wanted imports to have equal footprint and be identifiable, and those two goals don't need the same solution. Equal footprint, shelf grouping, done. One import that does want a mark is a file with no metadata — give it an ink spine with the filename in mono, which is honest rather than decorative.

Vertical text: keep it, with limits

The readability cost is real, so it gets bounded rather than argued away. Rotated 90° counter-clockwise, reading bottom-to-top, never stacked letters. Truncate to what fits on one line — a spine is a handle, not a citation. Author drops off below 44px. And hover or focus raises a horizontal label card, so the full title is one gesture away and nobody has to tilt their head. That, plus the Covers toggle for anyone who simply dislikes it, is enough.

Colours: derived, not generated

Two rules and no randomness. If the book has a cover, sample its dominant colour and snap it to the nearest of the eight hues from 9b — so Dorian Gray stays dark-gold-ish and the shelf still looks like your palette. If there's no cover, hash the book id into one of the eight. Fixed lightness and chroma, so a spine can never out-shout plum, and the same book is the same colour forever — that permanence is the whole reason a shelf works as memory.

Ordering

Open books first, and not as spines — two or three lying flat face-out in a “Continue” row, because the thing you're mid-way through deserves its cover and its position. Progress is a hairline fill at the foot of the object, never a percentage badge. Then the shelf, newest-added on the left. Height varies with page count within a 142–174px band, thickness 34–66px as before — two dimensions of variation is what makes a shelf scannable instead of a bar chart.

A disabled button with no explanation is a dead end — the user can see the exit and not the lock. One line naming the unmet condition converts a blocked state into an instruction, and it costs nothing.

On cursor: not-allowed

Keep it — it is the honest cursor, and it is the only affordance blind to colour. But pair it with pointer-events: auto on a wrapper so the button can still receive hover and show a tooltip; a genuinely inert element cannot explain itself.

Never opacity is not wrong in general — Material 3 prescribes exactly this, 38% on content and 12% on container, and WCAG exempts inactive controls from contrast minimums, so Google is within the rules. But note separately: Material fades the label and the fill as two tokens and removes elevation outright. Fading the composited element is the part that fails, because the shadow and border go with it and the button dissolves into the panel — and 38% of a gradient stays the most saturated thing on the screen. Ten blades is ten units, so the mark is a natural discrete gauge — each blade snaps in with a 180ms bloom as you clear a card. Right for anything countable: a review session, onboarding, the words in a chapter.

And yes — static emblem, blades filling. Not the unfold. The unfold is a transition and reads as "something is happening"; a gauge has to be readable at rest, and an animating mark can't be. Keep them separate: unfold for load and discover, blades for progress.

Don't use it for reading progress on a book — 10% granularity is too coarse for 300 pages, and a novel is continuous rather than countable. Keep the linear bar there, and put the blades where the unit is a card.
